# Will, Response, and the Loss of Perception

**Record type:** Independent investigation  
**Proposed repository location:** `investigations/`  
**Status:** Open — not a canonical amendment and not a completed Triple Constraint Law (TCL) determination.

**Source boundary:** This record is commentary and analysis. It does not modify `SYSTEM_MANIFEST.md`, the foundational GUA-W architecture, or the KJV biblical source text.

## 1. Investigative Question

**What authority can legitimately be assigned to the human will?**

A related working proposition has emerged:

> The human will may be understood, at one level, as a capacity to respond to what is presented, in a manner analogous to a plant responding to sunlight.

This analogy is a starting point for investigation, not an equation between plant response and human moral agency.

The central distinction to test is:

**The capacity to respond does not, by itself, confer authority to originate the source, establish the governing reference, or determine the truth by which the response is judged.**

## 2. Scriptural Sequence Under Examination

### 2.1 Provision precedes the boundary — Genesis 2:8–17 (KJV)

The sequence in Genesis 2 presents the garden and its provision before the stated prohibition:

- God places the man in the garden.
- God gives the man permission to eat freely of the trees.
- God identifies the tree of the knowledge of good and evil and gives a specific command concerning it.

The order matters to this investigation. The command is given within an already-provided environment.

**Question:** What does this order establish about the relationship between provision, command, human response, and authority?

### 2.2 The temptation and the human evaluation — Genesis 3:1–7 (KJV)

The serpent challenges the stated command and presents an alternative account of what eating will mean. Genesis 3:5 includes the promise that the humans' eyes will be opened and that they will be as gods, knowing good and evil.

Genesis 3:6 then records the woman's evaluation of the tree: it was good for food, pleasant to the eyes, and desired to make one wise. She takes and eats, and gives to her husband.

The order raises a question about reference:

- What had been stated by God?
- What was proposed by the serpent?
- What did the woman perceive, desire, and evaluate?
- Did the evaluation remain aligned with the given command, or did it treat the human evaluation as an independent reference?

The possibility that Genesis 3 describes a shift toward self-referential judgment is a hypothesis to examine, not a conclusion to impose on the text.

### 2.3 The aftermath — Genesis 3:7–13 (KJV)

After eating, the text records opened eyes, awareness of nakedness, the making of coverings, hiding, fear, and the exchange with God.

This sequence may be relevant to the claim that humanity's perception of reality changed. However, the precise nature and extent of that change require careful textual analysis.

The passage does not use the modern technical phrase “loss of perception.” That phrase must remain an interpretive description rather than a quotation or an established lexical definition.

## 3. The Plant-and-Sunlight Analogy

A plant responds to light under conditions it did not originate and does not author. Phototropic growth can serve as a limited illustration of response within an already-given order.

The analogy may help distinguish:

- **Source:** the light to which the plant responds.
- **Capacity:** the plant's biological capacity to respond.
- **Response:** the observable growth response.
- **Authority:** the plant does not thereby become the originator or author of the light or the conditions governing its response.

**Limitations:** Plant phototropism is a biological process. It does not establish that plants possess human-like awareness, intention, moral responsibility, or a will in the theological sense. Nor does it settle the nature of human willing.

The analogy is useful only for examining the narrower distinction between responding and originating.

## 4. Romans 2:4 — Goodness Leading to Repentance

Romans 2:4 (KJV) states:

> “...the goodness of God leadeth thee to repentance”

This verse introduces an important order for examination: God's goodness is described as leading toward repentance.

A working observation is:

**Provision and divine goodness precede the human response they lead toward.**

The phrase “The Answer comes first; the question is searching for it” is a conceptual shorthand for this proposed order. It is not a quotation from Romans and must not replace the verse's actual wording or context.

The surrounding passage, Romans 2:1–5, must also be considered. The text addresses judgment, God's goodness, and the hardness and impenitent heart. The verse should not be isolated to make a broader claim than its context supports.

## 5. Terms and Distinctions to Keep Separate

This investigation must not collapse the following distinctions:

| Distinction | Investigative question |
|---|---|
| Will / choice | Are these distinct concepts, and if so, how does the text distinguish them? |
| Capacity / authority | Does the ability to respond grant authority to establish the reference governing the response? |
| Source / response | What is originated, and what is derivative? |
| Given reference / self-reference | Does Genesis 3 depict a change in the reference used to evaluate what is good? |
| Perception / information | Is the issue lack of information, altered interpretation, misdirected desire, or a changed relationship to the Source? |
| Responsibility / origination | How does Scripture assign responsibility without making the human the original source? |

The Hebrew verb *yadaʿ* (ידע), commonly rendered “know,” has a range of uses. No complete theory of human perception, moral knowledge, or the will should be derived from the verb alone. Any lexical claim must be checked against the specific construction and context.

## 6. Preliminary Placement for Investigation Within GUA-W

The following are possible points of contact with existing GUA-W distinctions. They are not new architectural layers, operators, or canonical terms.

- **Author / Source:** God as the Author and originating Source, as stated in the existing manifest.
- **Word / Command:** the spoken command in Genesis 2:16–17.
- **Provision:** the environment and permission described before the prohibition.
- **Response:** the human response to the command and, later, to the serpent's alternative claim.
- **Reference:** the question of what governs the evaluation of good and evil.
- **Witness / Perception:** what is perceived, registered, interpreted, and acted upon.

No change to the established GUA-W sequence is proposed by this record. Its purpose is to investigate whether these distinctions clarify the scriptural sequence without adding to or silently revising the architecture.

## 8. Unresolved Questions

1. What does Genesis 3:5 mean in its immediate literary and theological context, and what does it not say?
2. Does Genesis 3:6 describe a change in the human reference for evaluating good and evil, or is another reading better supported by the text?
3. How should the “opened eyes” of Genesis 3:7 be related to the fear, hiding, and altered human-God encounter that follow?
4. What is the precise relationship between will, desire, evaluation, choice, and action in the passages under study?
5. How does Romans 2:4 function in its full argument in Romans 2:1–5?
6. How do John 6:37–40 and John 15:1–5 contribute to the question of divine initiative and derivative response?
7. Where does the plant analogy illuminate the distinction, and where does it cease to be valid?
8. Can “loss of perception” be stated more precisely without claiming that the text says more than it does?

## 9. Provisional Investigative Statement

The present working proposition is:

> A created being's capacity to respond does not make that being the originator of the source, the author of the governing order, or the final authority for determining the truth of its response.

Genesis 2–3 and Romans 2:4 provide passages for testing the order of divine provision, command, human evaluation, response, and consequence.

Whether Genesis 3 should be characterized as a loss of perception, a shift in reference, or a more complex combination remains unresolved.

This statement is provisional. It is not presented as a new GUA-W axiom, a final theological conclusion, or a scientific finding.
